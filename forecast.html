The Cognitive
Dividend

How AI is set to reshape Australia's economy by 2030 — and what it will take to make sure that change works for everyone.

Australia is at a turning point. AI could add 1.5–2.5% to GDP every year by 2030 — but capturing those gains means fixing how we train workers, regulate markets, and manage data, all at the same time. The real question is not whether AI will reshape the economy. It is whether the benefits will be shared broadly or end up in the hands of a few.

Economic Mechanisms

From model weights to GDP: the four transmission channels

AI does not hit the economy all at once. Its effects flow through four separate channels, each moving at a different speed and hitting different groups of workers and businesses.

The first channel is task automation. AI tools can now handle legal document review, medical image analysis, and financial reporting at a level that matches the average professional. The (OECD 2023) estimates 27% of Australian jobs are highly exposed to AI. That does not mean those jobs disappear — it means the routine parts get automated, freeing workers to focus on thinking, problem-solving, and working with people.

The second channel is productivity augmentation. When workers use AI tools alongside their own skills, output goes up significantly. (Noy & Zhang 2023) found that workers using ChatGPT finished tasks 40% faster and produced better results. (Goldman Sachs Economics Research 2023) projected that moderate AI adoption could lift global productivity by 1.5 percentage points per year — a boost similar in scale to what electrification delivered in the 1920s.

The third channel is creative destruction. AI makes many specialist tasks much cheaper to perform. This squeezes out the middlemen — businesses that existed mainly to provide access to expertise. At the same time, it opens up new markets for companies that offer AI-powered services. Businesses that move first and build up their own AI systems and data will have a lasting edge over competitors.

The fourth channel is distributional effect. Past waves of automation mostly hit factory workers and low-skill jobs. AI is different — it targets well-paid, office-based roles like financial analysts, junior lawyers, and software developers. These workers have always felt safe from automation. That is no longer the case. So the question of who benefits — and who loses out — matters just as much as whether the economy grows overall.

Speed of uptake matters as much as the technology itself. Because 97% of Australian businesses have fewer than 20 employees, it will take much longer for AI to spread here than in larger, more concentrated economies like the US — possibly 18 to 30 months behind. The tools are already available, but using them well requires good management and proper training. The technology alone cannot deliver the gains.

Sector Analysis

Not all sectors face the same future: Australia's exposure map

Australia's economy is built around mining and professional services. That shapes exactly which industries will feel AI's effects first — and hardest.

Financial services is the clearest example of AI cutting both ways. The sector makes up 8.5% of Australia's GDP and has a lot to gain — (KPMG Australia 2024) estimates AI could cut operating costs by 25–35% within five years through automating compliance and loan assessments. But the roles most at risk are also in this sector: junior analysts, compliance staff, and back-office workers. It is the industry most likely to benefit from AI and the one that will lose the most jobs to it.

Healthcare is where AI could do the most good. Australian companies like Harrison.ai and Annalise.ai have built diagnostic tools that match specialist doctors in reading X-rays and scans. For the five million Australians living in rural areas who struggle to access specialist care, this is not just about saving money — it is about getting people the help they actually need. The problem is that Australia's medical regulator, the TGA, was not set up to approve AI systems that keep learning and changing after they are released. That regulatory gap needs to be fixed.

Mining and resources is a sector where AI is already delivering real results. Rio Tinto and BHP have been running self-driving trucks and AI maintenance systems for years. Because mining sites are remote and dangerous, and the workforce is relatively small, these tools improve safety and cut costs without causing significant job losses. It is a case where AI is clearly adding value.

Aged care and construction are largely resistant to AI because their work is physical and deeply human. You cannot automate the care of an elderly person or the laying of foundations. This creates an uncomfortable gap: AI is disrupting well-paid city jobs while the industries that are genuinely short of workers — aged care, disability support, construction — are barely touched. Skills policy needs to help people move between these worlds.

Scenario Analysis

Three futures: what separates them is policy, not technology

All three scenarios start with the same AI technology. The difference between a good outcome and a bad one comes down to how quickly and how well Australia's institutions respond. And crucially, the choices that matter most need to be made in 2025–26 — before the full impact shows up in any economic data.

Policy Playbook

What to do, when, with whom — and what could go wrong

There is no single policy that fixes this. What is needed is a set of well-timed interventions that build up Australia's capacity to manage the transition before the disruption hits its peak.

The biggest risk is a timing mismatch. AI moves fast — capabilities shift every six to eighteen months. But government programs and regulations take four to eight years to develop and pass. Someone who loses their job in 2026 cannot wait until 2028 for a new TAFE course to be designed. And a startup that cannot get regulatory clarity will simply move to Singapore instead. Policy needs to move much faster than it normally does.

🎓
National AI Skills Compact
Immediate · 2025–2027
Build short-course programs at TAFEs and universities focused on skills that work alongside AI: understanding data, using AI tools effectively, and navigating AI governance. Aim for 300,000 completions by 2027, with businesses contributing through a levy system.
⚖ The cost is around A$2.4B upfront — but doing nothing risks far higher costs in unemployment support. Success measure: 65% of displaced workers back in comparable jobs within 24 months.
💸
SME AI Adoption Tax Credit
Immediate · 2025–2026
Give small businesses a 30% tax credit when they invest in AI tools — but only if they also invest in training their staff. The credit expires after four years so it does not become a permanent handout. Based on a model that has already worked in New Zealand.
⚖ Costs about A$800M a year in foregone tax revenue, but is estimated to generate A$3.2B in extra SME output. Target: AI adoption among small businesses reaches 65% by 2028, up from 34% today.
🔓
Open Government Data Framework
Near-term · 2025–2027
Make government data — health records, transport data, census information — openly available through standard digital access points. Partner with CSIRO and universities to use this data to build world-class AI tools in areas where Australia has a natural advantage, like healthcare and resources.
⚖ Requires careful handling of privacy and strong coordination across government agencies. Target: 500 high-quality datasets made available digitally by 2027, up from around 80 today.
⚖️
Adaptive AI Regulation
Near-term · 2026–2028
Create a regulation system where the rules are proportionate to the risk — stricter for high-stakes AI like medical tools, lighter for low-risk applications. Set up an office that can let new AI products be tested safely before full approval. Speed up the process for getting diagnostic AI cleared by the TGA.
⚖ Clear rules encourage investment, but set them too early and they may not age well. Target: approvals within 9 months for low-risk AI and 18 months for high-risk medical applications.
🌏
Indo-Pacific AI Trade Strategy
Medium-term · 2027–2030
Build Australia's reputation as a safe, trustworthy provider of AI for the region — particularly in health and resources. Sign data-sharing deals with Singapore, Japan, and South Korea. Australia's commitment to data privacy is an asset in a region wary of relying on either the US or China.
⚖ Navigating geopolitics is difficult, and Australia needs a mature domestic AI industry to export from. Target: AI exports grow from 2% to 8% of total services exports by 2030.
📋
AI-Aware Bargaining Framework
Medium-term · 2026–2029
Change workplace laws so companies have to assess the impact on workers before rolling out major AI changes. Trial agreements where businesses share the productivity gains with their employees. Fix the grey area around gig workers who power AI-adjacent platforms but miss out on basic protections.
⚖ If designed badly, this could discourage businesses from adopting AI at all. Target: workers' share of income in AI-heavy industries stays within 2 percentage points of today's level by 2030.

Unintended Consequences

Even good policies can backfire. Teaching people generic AI skills may not match what employers actually need in specific industries. A tax credit that does not require businesses to also train their workers could end up paying companies to automate jobs without actually making them more productive.

The most serious risk is regulatory arbitrage. If Australia's rules are stricter than those in Singapore or the US, companies will simply run their AI systems from overseas — still affecting Australian workers, but paying tax somewhere else. The answer is not lighter rules, but smarter ones: regulations that include built-in expiry dates and regular reviews so they can keep up with a fast-moving industry.

Methods, Data & Uncertainty

How this forecast was built — and what it cannot see

Any honest forecast has to acknowledge what it cannot know. This one is no different. There are six areas where the uncertainty is significant enough to affect the conclusions.

Model Uncertainty

The models used to estimate which jobs are at risk from AI are based on what AI can do today. But AI keeps improving quickly — what seemed safe 18 months ago may already be automated. These models almost certainly understate how exposed jobs will be in the future.

Adoption Uncertainty

Not every business will adopt AI at the same pace — it depends a lot on the quality of leadership and how well-run the organisation is. Businesses that hold off for too long may find themselves permanently behind, as the companies that moved early build up advantages that are hard to catch up with.

Measurement Gaps

Official statistics from the ABS are always 1–2 years behind, and they often miss improvements in quality rather than quantity. This means the benefits of AI may already be showing up in workplaces but not yet in the numbers — a pattern that happened with computers in the 1990s too.

Structural Break Risk

All three scenarios assume nothing catastrophic happens. A major AI safety failure, a technology split between the US and China, or a dramatic political shift in Australia could each completely change the picture in ways this forecast cannot predict.

Distributional Data Gaps

There is very little Australian-specific research on who will win and who will lose from AI. Most studies use data from the US and apply it here — but Australia has a bigger services sector and very different workplace laws, which means the US findings may not translate well.

General Equilibrium Effects

This forecast looks at each channel in isolation. In reality, changes in one part of the economy ripple through to others — lower costs in financial services affect what small businesses pay for loans, and so on. Those second-order effects are not modelled here, and they could make the overall impact either much bigger or much smaller.
